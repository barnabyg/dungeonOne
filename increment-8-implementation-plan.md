## 1. Intended playable result

Deliver one complete, authored, text-first adventure that an unfamiliar player can enjoy for roughly **2–4 hours** across more than one sitting. They can understand the opening problem, investigate competing accounts, choose routes and allies, survive or avoid several distinct threats, see earlier decisions change later scenes, and reach a satisfying ending. Command mode must remain fully playable without an API key; live AI mode should make ordinary-language play easier without deciding facts or rules.

This plan is a review input for a later `to-tickets` pass. It is not a claim that the adventure or its mechanics already exist. The roadmap's exit criterion is behavioral: external players finish and want another adventure. Duration, content count, automated reachability, and model scores are supporting evidence, not substitutes for that result.

## 3. Adventure brief to validate before expanding mechanics

Working concept: **The Hollow Beacon**. A valley's signal beacon has gone dark just before a caravan enters a dangerous pass. The watch captain wants it lit immediately. A refugee group fears the familiar signal has been compromised and will guide raiders to them. The signal keeper is missing. The player must discover who altered the beacon, decide whom to trust, and choose how to warn or shelter the people in the pass. This is a proposed creative brief, not canon; the first delivery slice should playtest its hook and replace it if it lacks tension.

Use a new content ID and title, with no imported chapel or crossroads save. Aim for **10–14 purposeful locations**, **five or six voiced NPCs**, **three distinct combat encounters** (at least one optional and one avoidable by evidence, alliance, or a physical route), a few useful items, and **three truthful end states**. These are design budgets, not quotas to fill with empty rooms. Every location needs a reason to visit, a discoverable lead, a consequential choice, a changed revisit, or a credible shortcut.

### Three-act player path

1. **The call for help (first 15–25 minutes).** Establish the caravan deadline, two conflicting requests, a visible clue, and at least two first leads. Let a player decline, ask questions, or leave with a truthful outcome. Teach the interface through one real action, not a long help screen.
2. **Investigation and pressure (roughly 60–120 minutes).** The watch post, road, beacon approach, and refugee route form a connected map with shortcuts. Evidence can be gathered in different orders. A false accusation or failed social check changes trust without erasing the physical evidence route. The clock advances on meaningful travel or deliberate waiting; local inspection and combat rounds do not consume whole days. Revisited places reflect rescues, deaths, offers, barricades, and the beacon's status.
3. **Confrontation and resolution (roughly 40–70 minutes).** The player can expose the altered signal and arrange a safe warning, rely on a slower human messenger, or commit to an urgent but risky signal. An avoidable confrontation and a late path keep the finale playable after missed leads, a lost item, a failed check, or an unavailable NPC. The ending states what happened to the caravan, refugees, keeper, and raiders according to recorded facts; it does not claim repairs, safety, or justice that the player did not achieve.

Draw a one-page location graph and timeline before writing the full file. For each ending, record its prerequisite facts, independent evidence path, allowed casualties, clock stage, and a seeded route witness. Give every essential clue at least two means of discovery (for example, a person and a physical record). Identify the shortest route, a curious route, a failed-check route, a late route, and a route that avoids an optional fight. Ask a reviewer who has not read the implementation to play a paper or command-only story outline; cut locations or mechanics that add length without a decision.

## 4. Rules to add only where the adventure requires them

**Keep one Fighter for this release unless early player testing demonstrates that character choice is necessary to make the adventure enjoyable.** Do not add Rogue, Cleric, Wizard, a spell list, or a full skill catalog as a checklist. Present the Fighter as a deliberate protagonist with a readable sheet: HP, defense, attack, equipment, limited resources, current conditions, and a short explanation of each available combat action. If a later class is justified, it needs its own complete start-to-ending route and balance pass.

Design the three encounters on paper first: a short introductory threat, a middle encounter with a meaningful environmental or social alternative, and a finale whose form depends on evidence or alliances. For each, specify why fighting happens, what can be learned, what the player can do besides repeating `attack`, retreat or surrender policy, defeat consequence, and where recovery is available. A minimal likely combat slice is **one defensive or positional choice**, **one scene-specific interaction**, and **a bounded recovery opportunity** between major encounters. The engine must own turns, costs, dice, damage, conditions, and encounter effects. Keep a single active opponent initially if these encounters remain varied; add simultaneous opponents only if playtest evidence shows the planned scenes cannot work honestly without them.

Generalize items just enough to carry quest evidence, an optional tactical resource, and a healing resource with distinct legal uses. Item placement, ownership, spending, and recovery must remain canonical; an AI interpretation may select a visible legal use but cannot invent an item effect. If equipment selection is offered, limit it to one or two meaningful choices with visible tradeoffs and exact saved stats. Avoid currency, encumbrance, crafting, and a general loot economy.

Use the existing day clock idea, but give the new tuple **explicit costs per journey or action type**. Local talk, search, and combat rounds should not make a day pass by default; crossing a mapped long route or waiting can. Make thresholds observable through plausible sources. A missed deadline changes the result but never removes all endings. The current schema-9 values, including its attack cost, retain their meaning. Any new time, item, combat, or ending semantics require a new schema/rules/engine identity and exact replay routing; old saves and embedded content remain readable.

## 5. Player interface and start/continue flow

The terminal needs a compact, scannable rhythm: **where you are and what changed; who and what is visible; current risk and clock; one or two leads; exact mechanics after an action; then the prompt**. Keep DM narration visually distinct from authoritative rolls, HP, item cost, clock change, and journal updates. The journal should separate observed evidence, testimony, an NPC's belief, open questions, and next leads. A short character sheet and inventory must be readable during combat and after resume. NPC dialogue should show the speaker and the subject being discussed, and `talk <person>` should reliably lead to copyable, currently legal topics in command mode.

For this game, provide a clear built-in adventure selector and a local save-slot flow (for example, list/start/continue by slot name), with an explicit seed displayed for reproducibility. Prevent accidental replacement of an occupied slot and make a completed slot visibly complete. Retain explicit `--adventure-file`, `--save`, and `--resume` for compatibility and testing. Consider changing the default startup adventure only after the new one passes clean-checkout and player gates; earlier tests and historical traces must retain their pinned adventure identity.

Treat a rejected or ambiguous command as editable input: give a precise reason and a valid next command without spending time, a die, or an item. Permit retry of an uncommitted model or UI failure; never reroll or reverse a committed attack, check, or consequence under the name of “undo.” If narration fails after commit, show deterministic authoritative output and keep the slot resumable. A player switching from command mode to AI mode should be able to continue the same save; record the present mixed-mode trace replay limitation until it is explicitly resolved.

AI mode should project only visible people, items, routes, facts, and allowed actions. Prompt/context growth over a multi-hour game needs a bounded, speaker-scoped history plus durable summaries derived from state, not a transcript that becomes a second source of truth. Test model latency, clarification frequency, costs, false claims, and recovery over a whole journey. If live AI becomes unavailable, the player can continue in command mode from the same committed save.

## 6. Delivery slices for a later `to-tickets` pass

These are dependency and review boundaries, not GitHub issues. Each slice should end in a player-visible demonstration. Split slices further only when the smaller result remains independently playable and testable.

1. **Freeze the game brief and prove the opening.** Use the completed increment-7 contract as the baseline. Produce the premise, location graph, timeline, scene/choice matrix, combat designs, ending truth table, content budget, shortest route, and paper-play findings. Build a small playable opening whose hook and route alternatives can be reviewed without reading JSON.
2. **Build a chapter-one vertical slice.** A new versioned authored file starts from a clear choice, offers two leads, supports one conversation and one investigation, records a consequence, saves, resumes, and shows the changed scene. Command and scripted-AI routes use the same state contract. This tests whether the proposed story invites exploration before bulk authoring.
3. **Make travel and the clock fit the story.** Add per-route or otherwise explicit travel costs in a new tuple, ordered one-time thresholds, truthful off-screen information, and a viable late route. Show the player a short and long way to the beacon and a noticeable but recoverable deadline change. Verify save/resume on either side of a threshold.
4. **Make clues, dialogue, and choices form an investigation.** Add the remaining NPCs and redundant clue sources, independent lead ordering, one failed social route, actor-specific knowledge, and revisits that change. Demonstrate a player reaching the same central inference through two different paths and correcting a mistaken belief from evidence.
5. **Make several encounters worth playing.** Add the minimum combat action/recovery rules selected from the encounter designs and implement three differentiated threats. Show an optional fight, a noncombat avoidance, a defeat/recovery or terminal policy, and a mid-combat restart. Balance against multiple seeds without hiding a required clue behind a lucky roll.
6. **Make items and final choices consequential.** Add only the required non-healing item uses and any narrowly chosen equipment option. Implement the final confrontation and three distinct truthful outcomes. Show how a spent item, a saved or lost NPC, late arrival, and an avoided fight change the finale without making it unreachable.
7. **Polish the whole terminal journey.** Implement scannable scene/conversation/combat layouts, a useful character sheet and journal, command recovery, built-in start selection, and safe local slots. An unfamiliar player should be able to start, understand a `talk` prompt, save, close, continue, and finish without developer coaching.
8. **Qualify live AI over full play.** Run scripted and selected live journeys through the same content, with ordinary phrasing, ambiguity, provider failure before and after a commit, long history, and command/AI save continuation. Correct false claims or repeated clarification loops and preserve exact model/prompt/tool/content evidence. This slice qualifies a particular model and content version, not arbitrary generated adventures.
9. **Qualify enjoyment and release readiness.** Conduct blind external sessions, repair observed blockers, repeat the affected player paths, run the canonical verification, and prove install/start/save/resume/replay from a clean checkout. Only then decide whether this adventure becomes the normal startup choice and record the final handoff.

Slices 3–6 share schema/runtime and content seams and should be integrated serially even if research or editorial review proceeds concurrently. Every implementation ticket needs a focused test of its player-visible result; full verification is required before a pushed series or release claim. Preserve separate commits per ticket under repository guidance.

## 7. Evidence and exit gates

### Automated and manual route evidence

- Keep named, seeded **CLI journeys** for the short, exploratory, late, failed-check, optional-combat-avoidance, casualty, and each ending route. Assert public scene, journal, inventory, HP, clock, NPC knowledge, ending text, and RNG position at key points. Test an actual input → parser or DM tool → rules → save → process restart → output path, including a restart mid-combat and near a clock threshold.
- Test invalid, ambiguous, hidden, stale, repeated, and compound attempts for zero unauthorized state changes. Test old adventures, save formats, trace formats, and pinned tuples. Test a source-free resume and diagnostic replay of new-format same-mode segments. If mixed-mode replay is added, verify linked command-to-AI and AI-to-command segments; otherwise state the limitation in player and maintainer guidance.
- Run a balance matrix across several documented seeds and main route classes. Record forced defeats, softlocks, unused clues, repeated attacks, time pressure, and recovery resources. Do not claim that seed-0 success proves fair play. Ensure failure or refusal has a specific continuation unless the player knowingly chooses a terminal ending.
- Run `npm.cmd run verify` with all seven ordered gates and zero warnings. From a clean checkout, run `npm.cmd ci`, verification, build, a fresh start, a committed save, a process restart, an ending, and replay using only tracked inputs plus artifacts produced by that run.

### Experience evidence

- Before final content lock, conduct a short blind opening test. Record where players think they should go, what they try, and when they first feel they made a meaningful choice. Revise the hook or interface before multiplying content if they cannot find a lead.
- For the final candidate, recruit **at least three unfamiliar external players** spanning command and live AI mode. Do not show the JSON, route witnesses, or preferred choices. Record actual elapsed time, mode/model/seed/content version, start and stop points, saves/resumes, exact confusing inputs and responses, hint use, chosen path and ending, contradictions, and whether they would voluntarily play another adventure. Ask what they believed each major choice changed. Keep raw saves/traces private because they contain hidden facts and player text.
- The provisional release gate is: no authoritative-state contradiction or unfinishable supported branch in tested play; at least two players reach a truthful ending without story coaching; completed sessions plausibly fall near the 2–4 hour target without padded travel; and at least two independently say they would choose another adventure. Treat negative or thin evidence as a revision request, not as a passed milestone. If three players are insufficient to resolve a disputed result, recruit more rather than weakening the criterion.
- Review at least one full live-AI journey, not only isolated turns, for tool choice, narration truth, secret withholding, clarification loops, latency, and cost. Exact model, prompt/tool/content/rules versions and sanitized evidence belong in the acceptance record. Scripted-AI tests prove orchestration only.

The implementation handoff should provide a copyable PowerShell command with a concrete adventure, seed, and save path; separate in-game commands and expected branch responses; and an exact resume command after waiting for the next `>` prompt and pressing Ctrl+C. State prerequisites and untested modes. `quit` closes the saved session.

## 8. Explicit boundaries and decisions for ticket planning

Do not turn this into a full D&D rules implementation, a generated-content project, a campaign import, a graphical UI, multiplayer, tactical-grid combat, unrestricted model-authored state changes, or a rewrite of old adventure contracts. The new game may be more polished than previous fixtures while retaining small deterministic mechanics.

Resolve these decisions after the first blind opening and encounter prototypes, before committing to broad implementation tickets:

1. Does **The Hollow Beacon** create a clear emotional stake and two genuinely attractive routes, or should the brief be replaced?
2. Which one combat choice and recovery rule actually make the planned encounters more interesting? Is simultaneous-opponent combat necessary, or can it stay outside this increment?
3. What is the minimum non-healing item vocabulary and exact travel-cost model for this story?
4. Does a local slot selector improve real startup/resume usability enough to ship now, and should the qualified game replace the current default?
5. Which increment-7 player-facing lessons should shape the opening of the longer adventure?

Choose these from observed play and executable examples. Any proposal that makes released saves or content unreadable requires explicit approval, affected-version identification, and a migration plan before implementation.
